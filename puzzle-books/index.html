What one solution means here

Hard to see, not hard to guess.

A puzzle book can say unique solution and mean only that somebody checked the answer once. We mean something narrower. Each puzzle is built by a program that also solves it. The solver is asked to find two solutions; the puzzle is kept only if it finds exactly one, and only if it reached that one by applying rules, never by putting in a number to see whether the grid falls apart later.

That is why the hard puzzles here are long rather than lucky. It also means the answer at the back is the answer, not one of several that happen to fit.

Two more things the program checks before a book is printed: no two puzzles in a book are the same, and no two have the same answer either. Difficulty rises through the book, from a small easy grid to the full sized challenge ones at the end, so the twenty fifth puzzle is harder than the first without a jump in the middle.

Everything is drawn as vector line work, not as pictures of screens: the lines stay sharp at any print size, the numbers are set in a plain sans face, and there is no grey that a home printer has to guess at.

Questions

Before you pay.

What exactly do I get, and in which formats?

Two PDF files for each book you buy: one laid out for A4 and one for US Letter. The puzzles are the same in both, only the page size differs, so you print whichever suits your paper. Both are black and white, with the fonts embedded, between 78 and 120 pages depending on the book, and between 0.4 and 4 MB. There is no DRM and no account: the files are yours to keep.

How do I print it?

On any home or office printer, in black and white. Choose the file that matches your paper, print at 100 percent or actual size rather than fit to page, and the grids come out at the size they were drawn. You can print a few pages at a time, print one page twice if you spoil it, or have a copy shop run the whole book. The solutions are all at the back, so you can leave them in the drawer.

Which ten kinds of puzzle are these?

Slitherlink (Otters), Nonograms (Magpies), Kakuro (Squirrels), Hashi or Bridges (Cranes), Masyu (Swans), Nurikabe (Voles), Killer Sudoku (Badgers), Numberlink (Herons), Anti-knight Sudoku (Hares) and Star Battle (Hedgehogs). Each book holds one kind and nothing else. The rules are printed in the front of every book with one worked example, and you can also read them and play a puzzle a day free at arling.sk/games.

How do you know every puzzle has exactly one solution?

Because a program checked all one thousand of them. The solver is run with a limit of two solutions; if it finds a second one, the puzzle is thrown away and another is generated. It also has to reach the solution by rules alone, without trial and error. On top of that the program re-derives every clue from the printed answer and compares it with the printed puzzle, so a drawing mistake cannot slip in either.
