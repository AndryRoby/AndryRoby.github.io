Rules.

Three sentences. The rest is practice.

How to play

Every row and every column has a list of numbers: the lengths of its blocks of filled cells, in order, with at least one empty cell between two blocks. A filled cell means a magpie left something shiny there; an empty cell means nothing. Work out, from the numbers alone, which cells must be filled and which must be empty.

The grid size changes through the week, from eight by eight on Monday to fifteen by fifteen on Sunday. Every picture has exactly one arrangement of filled cells that fits every number.

A cross is your own note that a cell stays empty. When every number is satisfied, the game checks the picture by itself.

Controls

Tap or click a cell: once fills it, twice puts a cross, three times clears it. Hold the button or your finger and drag to sweep the same mark over many cells at once. Nothing lights up while you play: finding a mistake is part of the puzzle.

Check first tells you only how many marks are wrong. Press it again and it shows which. Hint outlines the row or column it is looking at, marks what follows and explains why; press it again to fill it in. Hints are counted and shown when you finish. Clear empties the grid; the clock keeps running.

Keyboard: arrows move, Space or Enter cycles the cell, F fill, X cross, Delete or Backspace clears, P pauses. Undo takes back the last move.

The clock runs from your first move to the last cell and pauses when you leave the tab, which also hides the grid. Your progress, time, history and day streak stay in this browser, and in your account when you are signed in, so you can continue on another device.

The week

Monday and Tuesday are easy (eight by eight), Wednesday and Thursday medium (ten by ten), Friday and Saturday hard (twelve by twelve) and Sunday is the challenge: fifteen by fifteen. The week strip under the grid takes you to the other days; the archive goes back a year, and the practice sets have eight pictures of each level with no date attached.

Difficulty is measured, not guessed: every picture is checked to be solvable by line logic alone (no guessing), and the candidates for a date are ranked by how many settling passes that logic needs. Easy days settle fast; Sunday needs the most passes.

A few ways in

A block bigger than half the line always overlaps itself: work out its leftmost and rightmost position and mark what both agree on.

Once a line's numbers add up (with their gaps) to exactly the line's length, every block's position is forced: no other arrangement fits.

A cross you place narrows the room left for a block, sometimes forcing more cells around it; a filled cell you place can rule out arrangements that miss it.

About this game.

Every picture is generated by our own program from its date: new every day and the same for everyone. The program only accepts a picture that pure row-and-column logic can solve completely on its own, without ever having to guess. No picture is taken from anywhere else. The mechanic itself is a classic picture-logic puzzle known as a nonogram under several names; this implementation, the daily pictures, the hints and the words are ours.

Your grid, time, history and day streak are stored only in this browser. Our anonymous statistics (self-hosted Umami) record the page visit, as on every page of ours, and a few extra events: that someone solved a picture and in how many seconds, and that Check or Hint was used. Nothing else.

Guide

How to play Magpies.

Three rules, one picture a day, exactly one solution. The pictures are made to be reasoned out, not guessed: the numbers by every row and column are always enough, no guessing required.

Play today's picture Start with an easy one

The rules.

1. Numbers by every row and every column. Each number is the length of one block of filled cells, in order, with at least one empty cell between two blocks. A grid with eight rows has eight columns; the size changes through the week, from eight by eight on Monday to fifteen by fifteen on Sunday.

2. A filled cell means a magpie left something shiny there. An empty cell means nothing. Work out, from the numbers alone, which is which.

3. A cross is your own note that a cell stays empty. Crosses are yours; the game does not judge them, except when you ask with Check. When every number is satisfied, the game checks the whole picture by itself.

Crosses are notes, not answers. Placing a cross narrows the room left for a block in that line; the game never marks a cross wrong while you play, only when you ask with Check. On the phone, hold your finger and drag to sweep the same mark over several cells. On a keyboard, arrows move, F fills, X crosses, Delete clears, P pauses.

A cross you were wrong about is shown by Check, never by the board turning red while you play.

A way in.

Every picture opens with steps like these. Hint finds the next one for you, outlines the row or column it is looking at, marks the cells that follow and says why; press it again to fill them in. Hints are counted and shown when you finish, never hidden.

  • Forced overlap. A block bigger than half the line must overlap itself wherever it goes: work out its leftmost and rightmost position and mark what both agree on.
  • Exactly enough room. When a line's numbers, with a gap between each, add up to exactly the line's length, every block's position is forced.
  • A full line. Once a row or column's numbers are satisfied, every other cell in it is a cross.
  • Marks already placed. Crosses and filled cells you placed narrow what still fits; Hint reasons from them the same way it reasons from the numbers alone.

The week.

Difficulty is measured, not guessed. Every picture is checked to be solvable by line logic alone; the candidates for a date are ranked by how many settling passes that logic needs.

Monday, TuesdayEasy, eight by eight. The numbers open the picture up fast.
Wednesday, ThursdayMedium, ten by ten.
Friday, SaturdayHard, twelve by twelve. More passes before it opens up.
SundayChallenge, fifteen by fifteen, the most passes of the week's candidates.
