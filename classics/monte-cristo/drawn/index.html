The Count of
Monte Cristodrawn in code

A young sailor is betrayed on the day of his wedding, spends fourteen years in a fortress on a rock, and comes back rich enough to repay every kindness and every wrong. Here is his story in 37 scenes, each printed like a risograph poster and each with the words from the book it was drawn from.

Every picture on this page is made by code in your browser as you scroll: shapes laid down in three inks, screened into dots and printed slightly out of register, the way a risograph drum prints on paper. No photograph, no image file, no picture generator.

I

Marseilles, 1815

February 1815. A young sailor comes home to be made captain and to marry, and three men decide otherwise.

II

The Château d’If

Fourteen years in a fortress on a rock off Marseilles, and the old prisoner in the next cell who teaches him everything he knows.

III

The sea, the island, the debt

Out of the sea, on to a smugglers’ ship, into the cave of a cardinal’s treasure, and back to Marseilles to repay a kindness.

IV

Rome and the carnival

Nine years later, two young Parisians in Rome meet a count who seems to know every bandit and every secret of the city.

V

Paris and Auteuil

1838. The count takes a house in Paris and another at Auteuil, and one by one the people who sent Edmond Dantès to the Château d’If come to his table.

VI

Janina and the challenge

A girl’s memory of a lake in Greece brings down a peer of France, his son calls out the count, and in the night Mercédès comes to the count’s house.

VII

The reckoning

Paris settles its accounts: a bride leaves dressed as a young man, a false prince hides on a roof, a sick girl is watched over at night, a funeral, and a trial in which a son names his father.

VIII

Wait and hope

The count leaves Paris, goes back to Marseilles and to his own cell in the Château d’If, forgives the last of the three men, and on the island where his fortune began leaves two words behind him.

Wait and hope.

The last words of the novel, in the count’s letter and then on Valentine’s lips: Fac et spera.

How this page is made

Each scene is a short program. It lays down shapes in three separate inks, sunflower yellow, bright red and medium blue, as if cutting three stencils. The page then turns each ink into dots at its own angle, gives the ink an uneven texture with a few pinholes where the drum missed, and prints the three over each other on paper, slightly out of register, the way a risograph does. There is no image file, no photograph and no picture generator behind any of them.

The code was written by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, for ARLing, and every scene was checked against the text and on screenshots before it went up.

The quotations are word for word from the English translation of 1846 as published by Project Gutenberg (eBook #1184), which is in the public domain. Places, clothes, disguises and light follow what the book says. Where the book is silent, for instance on the colour of a dress, we chose, and the caption says so.

The book has hard moments: prison, poison and death. We show them only by a sign, a lantern on a rampart or a ring on the water, never the thing itself.

A picture is printed only when you scroll near it, in a background thread, so scrolling stays smooth; you see the three inks pass over the paper once, and after that nothing on the page moves or runs. If your device asks for reduced motion, the pictures simply appear.
